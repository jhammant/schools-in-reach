## Who pays for what

- **The school** pays for support from its own budget up to a point. A mainstream school is expected to pay the first £6,000 a year of extra support for a pupil with special needs.
- **The council** tops up costs above £6,000 a year from its high needs budget, and pays for places in special schools.
- Across England, about 5.3% of pupils now have an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP), up from just below 3% in the past.

## The Dedicated Schools Grant

Councils receive government funding for schools, early years and high needs through the Dedicated Schools Grant (DSG). It has four blocks: schools, central school services, early years and high needs. The DfE says the vast majority of councils' DSG deficits come from spending on high needs, but in accounting terms there is no separate high needs deficit: the deficit is on the DSG as a whole.

School Money's council pages show each council's DSG balance at the end of each year, from the DfE's section 251 outturn returns. A negative balance is a deficit.

## Questions to ask

- How many pupils at the school have an EHCP or need extra support, and how is support above the first £6,000 a year funded?
- Does the council's top-up money arrive on time and cover what the support actually costs?
- Is the council's high needs budget in deficit, and could that affect the school's top-ups?
- What training do staff get in supporting pupils with special needs? The IFS says reform would likely involve building mainstream schools' ability to support pupils with high needs, for instance through more teacher training (IFS 2026 report, p. 61).

## What helps

A school with many pupils who need extra support can feel it in its budget even when it is well run. When you see a deficit, look at the school's special needs funding on its page, and at the council's DSG balance, before drawing conclusions.
